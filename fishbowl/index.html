14Four ventures · what repeated

Four ventures in. Here's what kept happening.

Mistake I kept makingStarting from the technologyI'd find something I could build, then go looking for someone who needed it. That order is backwards and it costs years.
Mistake I kept makingConfusing interest with demand"That's fascinating, send me more" is not a buying signal. It's politeness. I treated it as a pipeline for a decade.
What I stopped doingBuilding before scoringNow every direction gets scored against the same rubric before a line of code — you'll see the 13-area version later in this deck.
What I started doingFinding the budget holder firstBefore the product. Before the deck. Who has the money, and does this problem already hurt them enough to spend it?
The pattern took four companies to see. You get it for free in the next forty minutes.

Concept 01Problem / solution fit

You don't have fit because people like it.

Signals that feel like fit
  • "This is so cool"
  • Waitlist signups
  • Press and social engagement
  • Investors asking follow-up questions
  • Friends using it for free
Signals that are fit
  • They describe the problem to you before you describe it to them
  • They are already paying for a bad solution
  • They have a budget line for it this fiscal year
  • They ask about implementation, not features
  • They get annoyed when the conversation ends without a next step
If you have to explain why it hurts, it doesn't hurt enough yet.

RealityThe recommendation is a feature anyone can copy

What we hadA garment graph, embeddings, and — critically — outcome data: what people kept, what came back, and the stated reason why. Nobody in apparel systematically connects those three.
What that impliedThe entity with the pain, the budget and the data obligation isn't the shopper. It's the retailer. Same technology. Entirely different company.
A pivot isn't abandoning what you built. It's realizing you pointed it at the wrong buyer.
Concept 02B2B2C — the seductive middle

Our first move was B2B2C. Here's why that's the hardest model there is.

The pitch to yourself"We keep the consumer magic and let retailers pay for it."You get the brand you love and the revenue you need. It sounds like a solution to everything.
The realityYou now have two customers and two productsA consumer experience that must delight, and an enterprise product that must survive procurement. Pre-seed, you cannot staff both.
When it does workWhen one side is nearly freeB2B2C works when the consumer side needs no acquisition spend — distribution comes from the business. If you're buying both audiences, it's two companies.
B2B2C is where founders go when they aren't ready to give up the consumer dream. I was there for a year.
Concept 03Customer discovery, done properly

Three different people. Only one of them has money.

The userLives in the workflowReturns managers, merchandising and fit teams, ops analysts. They'll tell you whether it's usable. They cannot buy it.
The championCarries it internallyThe person who spends their own political capital to push it through. You need one. They still can't sign.
The buyerOwns the budgetVP/Director of e-commerce, retail ops, CX or reverse logistics. One question decides everything: will you pay to fix this?
I spent the better part of a year doing excellent discovery with users and calling it validation. Users will enthusiastically validate a product nobody will ever purchase.
Validate the buyer and the budget first. The workflow, the channel and the stack follow.

Concept 04The wedge

A wedge is the smallest thing you can be undeniably best at.

A wedge must be
  • Narrow — one painful, specific, budgeted problem
  • Winnable — you can be clearly best, not marginally better
  • Urgent — it hurts this quarter, not someday
  • Expandable — it opens an adjacent door once you're inside
  • Defensible — doing it gives you an asset competitors can't copy
A wedge is not
  • Your favorite feature
  • The biggest market you can name
  • The hardest thing you can build
  • A smaller version of the whole vision
  • Anything you chose without scoring the alternatives
Narrow entry. Wide expansion. Founders fall in love with the expansion and skip the entry.

25Sartori · the wedge

Returns won. Here's the scorecard it won on.

NarrowOne workflow, one owner, one number on a P&L.
WinnableIncumbents report that a return happened. Nobody diagnoses why.
UrgentIt's margin, it's measured monthly, and it's getting worse.
ExpandableRoot cause reaches back into fit, sizing, PDP, buying and design.
DefensibleEvery return we diagnose makes the outcome graph better. Data compounds.
And the quiet one: it's the only direction where the thing we already built — the garment graph and the outcome data — is the actual moat rather than a nice-to-have.
We didn't pick returns because we loved it. We picked it because it scored.

Concept 05Validation vs. vanity

Set the threshold before you run the interviews.

Problem difficulty18/20target ≥67% — cleared
Unprompted ID9/20target ≥42% — cleared
Credible pilot interest14/20target ≥33% — cleared
Concrete next step4/20target ≥17% — cleared
Why thresholds firstIf you decide what "good" means after you see the data, you will always find a way to call it good. Writing the bar down in advance is the only defense against your own optimism.
The strongest number hereUnprompted identification — 9 of 20 described the problem before we did. That one can't be led, coached or wished into existence. It's the hardest signal to fake and the one investors ask about.
Twenty honest interviews beat two hundred flattering ones.

Rule twoStages only move forward on their actionSomething they did — booked, submitted, signed. Not something you sent.

31Fundraising · the math nobody tells you

Let me be honest about what raising actually looks like.

The conversion realityIt is a low-single-digit yes rateAcross a serious target list. Not because the company is bad — because almost every fund has a thesis you don't fit, and you can't know which from the outside.
The time realityRaising is a full-time jobWhile the other full-time job — building and selling — does not pause. This is the actual difficulty, and nobody warns you.
The emotional realityYou will hear no, constantlyThe founders who make it aren't the ones who hear less no. They're the ones who built a system that makes no survivable.
The only antidote to rejection at volume is process.
32Fundraising · the system

So I built a system. Same discipline as the sales funnel.

Coverage127accelerator and program tracker — scored, deadlined, status-tracked
Coverage50target investor firms, researched against thesis fit before a single email
DisciplineStage + exit testEvery conversation sits in a stage with a defined next action and an owner. Nothing lives in my head.
What this buys youWhen a no arrives, it's a stage transition, not a verdict on your life's work. That reframe is worth more than any pitch coaching you'll ever get.
Treat fundraising like a pipeline and it stops being personal.
